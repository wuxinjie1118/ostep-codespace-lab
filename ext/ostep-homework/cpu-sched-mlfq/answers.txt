Q2.  Reproducing the examples of the chapter
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Fig 8.2 (single long-running job):
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -q 10 -l 0,200,0 -c
  The job enters the top queue Q2, runs one 10ms slice there, is demoted to
  Q1 after 10ms, and to Q0 after 20ms, where it stays until completion at
  t=200.  (Exactly the "Q2 -> Q1 -> Q0" staircase of the figure.)

Fig 8.3 (a short job arrives while a long job runs):
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -q 10 -l 0,180,0:100,20,0 -c
  Job0 (CPU-bound, 180ms) sinks to the bottom queue. Job1 arrives at t=100,
  is placed in the top queue Q2, runs only 2 slices (20ms) and FINISHES at
  t=120 before ever being demoted -- response 0, turnaround 20 -- then Job0
  resumes and finishes at t=200.  MLFQ approximates SJF for the short job.

Fig 8.4 (I/O-bound job vs CPU-bound job):
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -q 10 -l 0,100,0:0,100,1 -c
  Job1 issues an I/O every 1ms of CPU, so under the rules it keeps top
  priority (Q2) and preempts Job0, which sinks to Q1/Q0:
    t0-9  Job0 @Q2 | t10 Job1 @Q2, IO at t=11 | t11-15 Job0 @Q1
    t16   Job1 resumes @Q2 ... (alternating)
  Results: Job 0: response 0, turnaround 118   Job 1: response 10, turnaround 605
  Interactive job 1 stays at high priority thanks to its frequent I/O.

(The boost / gaming variants of Figs 8.5-8.7 are exercised in Q4/Q5 below.)

Q4.  Gaming the scheduler with the old Rules 4a/4b (-S)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Workload:  python mlfq.py -n 3 -S -I -i 1 -q 100 -l 0,500,99:0,500,0 -c
  Job 0: run 500ms, issues an I/O every 99ms (1ms before its 100ms slice ends)
  Job 1: CPU-bound, run 500ms, no I/O
  With -S (old Rules 4a/4b) Job0's tick/allotment counters are reset every
  time it issues an I/O, so it NEVER leaves the top queue; with -I it is
  re-inserted at the HEAD of the top queue right after each I/O completes.
  Result: over [0,505) Job0 runs 500 of 505ms = 99.0% of the CPU; Job1 gets
  only the 5 x 1ms I/O windows.  (Job0 DONE at 505, Job1 DONE at 1000.)
  Gaming principle: by issuing an I/O just before its slice ends, an I/O-bound
  job retains top priority forever and nearly monopolizes the CPU -- exactly
  the attack that the newer Rule 4 (allotment-based accounting) removes.

  Without -I the same -S workload still gives ~99% except for one early
  100ms window (Job1's first full slice before it is demoted):
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -S -i 1 -q 100 -l 0,9900,99:0,9900,0 -c
  --> over its lifetime Job0 holds ~98% of the CPU.

Q5.  Boost interval that guarantees >= 5% CPU to a starving long job
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Starvation scenario (old rules, two interactive jobs that alternate 1ms of
CPU and 1ms of I/O forever at the top queue):
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -q 10 -S -i 1 -l 0,1000,0:0,1000,1:0,1000,1 -c
  The long Job0 gets the CPU only for its initial 20ms, then 0ms from
  t=10 until t~2001: it is completely starved while interactive jobs hog Q2.

With a priority boost every 200ms:
  python mlfq.py -n 3 -q 10 -S -B 200 -i 1 -l 0,1000,0:0,1000,1:0,1000,1 -c
  Job0 runs exactly 10ms in every 200ms window -> 5.0% of the CPU
  (verified window by window: [0,200):10ms, [200,400):10ms, ... all 5.0%).

Answer: with quantum q = 10ms in the top queue, a boosted job is guaranteed
at least one full 10ms slice per boost period S, so we need 10/S >= 0.05,
i.e. S <= 200ms.  Boost at least every 200ms (-B 200), or in general
S <= q / 0.05 = 20*q.

Q6.  Effect of the -I flag (queue re-insertion policy after I/O)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Workload (two desynchronized I/O jobs at one priority level):
  python mlfq.py -n 1 -q 5 -i 3 -l 0,20,2:0,20,3 -c            (no -I)
  python mlfq.py -n 1 -q 5 -i 3 -l 0,20,2:0,20,3 -I -c         (with -I)

Without -I, a job that finishes an I/O is appended to the BACK of its
queue: at t=10 Job0's I/O completes, but Job1 is mid-slice at the head of
the queue and keeps running until its slice ends -- Job0 must wait.
  Results: Job 0: response 0, turnaround 52 | Job 1: response 2, turnaround 40

With -I, the job that finishes an I/O is inserted at the FRONT of its queue
and runs immediately: at t=10 Job0 runs right away (trace differs from
t=10 onward).
  Results: Job 0: response 0, turnaround 51 | Job 1: response 2, turnaround 47

Conclusion: -I favors I/O-bound jobs (they resume without waiting behind a
CPU-bound job's long slice), improving their response/turnaround, at the
expense of the CPU-bound job at the same level; without -I an I/O job can be
delayed behind the head-of-queue job's entire quantum.
