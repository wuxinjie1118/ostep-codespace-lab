Q2.  Extreme ticket imbalance: 1 ticket vs 100 tickets
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  python lottery.py -l 10:1,10:100 -c     (Job0: len 10, 1 ticket; Job1: len 10, 100 tickets)

Job0 almost never runs before Job1 completes.  Over seeds 0..22 (23 runs),
Job0 got a slice before Job1 finished in only ONE case (seed 19, where it
won 1 slice and Job1 finished at time 11); in the other 22 runs Job1 won
every lottery and finished first (times 10 or 11), and Job0 only started
afterwards (finishing at 20).

Probability: Job1 needs 10 slices; Job0 wins a given slice with probability
1/101, so P(Job0 runs at least once before Job1 finishes) =
  1 - (100/101)^10 =~ 0.095  (about 9.5%).

General effect of such a ticket imbalance: the 1-ticket job is effectively
starved over short intervals -- it is overwhelmingly likely to never run at
all before the 100-ticket job completes.  Lottery scheduling only
approximates the ticket proportions in the long run (law of large numbers);
over short horizons the small-ticket job can be completely shut out.

Q3.  Fairness of two equal jobs (100:100 tickets), quantum = 1
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  python lottery.py -l 100:100,100:100 -s SEED -c
  (each job: run length 100, 100 tickets)

  seed 0: JOB0@192, JOB1@200  -> F = 192/200 = 0.960
  seed 1: JOB1@196, JOB0@200  -> F = 196/200 = 0.980
  seed 2: JOB1@190, JOB0@200  -> F = 190/200 = 0.950
  seed 3: JOB0@196, JOB1@200  -> F = 196/200 = 0.980
  seed 4: JOB1@199, JOB0@200  -> F = 199/200 = 0.995
  Average F =~ 0.973.

  So even with equal tickets the scheduler is noticeably unfair over this
  horizon: the "unlucky" job typically finishes 4-10ms (2-5%) earlier than
  the other; the second job always finishes at 200.  Fairness is only
  probabilistic and improves as jobs run longer (see Q5 graph).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Q4.  Effect of a larger quantum
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  python lottery.py -l 100:100,100:100 -q N -s SEED -c

  quantum 1 :  avg F ~ 0.973  (seeds 0-4)
  quantum 10:  seed0 150/200 -> 0.75 | seed1 160/200 -> 0.80
  quantum 50:  seed0 150/200 -> 0.75
  quantum 100: seed0 100/200 -> 0.50

  A larger quantum means fewer lottery draws, so the outcomes become
  coarser and less fair.  With quantum = 100 (equal to the job length),
  whichever job wins the FIRST lottery runs its whole 100ms to completion
  and finishes first, so F = 0.5 every time -- maximum unfairness.
  In general: smaller quanta -> more draws -> fairer (but more context-switch
  overhead).

Q5.  Fairness-vs-job-length graph (textbook Figure 9.2) and stride
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Replication:  fairness.py  (stdlib-only script that replicates lottery.py's
ticket selection exactly: r = int(random()*1000001), winner = r % total)
  Outputs:  fairness.csv  (data)   fairness_graph.svg  (plot)
  30 trials per job length R, two jobs with 100 tickets each, quantum 1:

  R      :   1     2     5    10    20    50   100   200   500  1000
  avg F  : 0.50  0.64  0.76  0.83  0.88  0.92  0.95  0.97  0.98  0.98

  Shape matches the textbook: fairness starts very low (R=1: whoever wins
  the single lottery finishes at t=1, the loser at t=2, so F=0.5) and
  asymptotically approaches 1 as the job length grows -- randomness delivers
  the ticket proportions only over the long run.

  Worth exploring further: more than two jobs; unequal ticket ratios and how
  fast realized shares converge to the ticket proportions; the variance of F
  (not just its mean).

  With a STRIDE scheduler the graph would be a flat line at F = 1.0 for every
  R: stride is deterministic.  Two jobs with equal tickets get equal strides,
  the scheduler always picks the job with the lowest pass value, so the two
  jobs alternate perfectly and finish at exactly the same time -- perfect
  fairness with zero variance, no matter how short the jobs are.  The cost:
  per-job pass state must be maintained (new jobs joining mid-run are
  awkward), whereas lottery scheduling is stateless and handles arrivals
  naturally.
